Construction timesheets: how to track labour hours and see if a job pays

You priced a job at 200 hours. In the end you used 250. You invoiced what you quoted, but you made far less than you thought. Construction timesheets are there to stop that happening.

The problem is not just the 50 extra hours. The problem is noticing once the job is finished, when there was nothing left to do. Tracking labour hours on site is for exactly this: noticing while there is still time.

Logging attendance is not enough

Knowing that four operatives worked eight hours tells you the gang total: 32 hours. But it does not tell you what each task cost.

To really control a job, every hour must be tied to two things:

  • the room or area: ground floor, bathroom 2, north elevation;
  • the task: framing, boarding, jointing, skimming.

Only then, halfway through, can you say "first-floor boarding is on track, skimming isn't".

The sum that makes the difference

Example with a labour cost of €30 an hour.
Task allowed: 200 hours → €6,000
Hours actually used: 250 hours → €7,500
Difference: €1,500 straight off your margin.

If halfway through you have already used 130 hours out of 200, you know you are heading for 260. That is when you can step in: find out what is slowing things down, move a person, talk to the client if the work has changed.

Where the extra hours come from

It is almost never someone who "can't be bothered". The most common causes are:

  • missing materials, with the gang standing idle or sent to the merchant;
  • errors or design changes;
  • work that has to be redone;
  • other trades in the same room;
  • changes asked for by the client, often verbally;
  • difficulties nobody could see at quoting stage.

If you log the reason along with the hours, you get two things: you know where to improve, and you have the evidence to claim variations when the cause is not down to you.

How to fill in timesheets without wasting time

  • Every day, not at the end of the week: from memory, hours always get "rounded".
  • One person logs them, the site manager or the owner, for the whole gang: fewer errors and no operative filling in forms.
  • The same tasks as the quote, so the comparison is instant.

The day's hours then go straight into the daily site report. To see how hours fit with the rest of the job, read construction site management.

In short

  • ✅ Hours tied to room and task, not just the person
  • ✅ Hours allowed / hours used compared during the job, not at the end
  • ✅ Reason for extra hours written down straight away
  • ✅ One person logs for the whole gang, every day
