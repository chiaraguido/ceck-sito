Construction programme: how to plan works without chasing deadlines

A construction programme is not just there to say when the job finishes. Above all, it shows what has to happen first so the next task can start.

Dependencies between tasks

The decorator can't finish a wall until the drylining fitter has finished. The joiner can't fit the unit until the floor is ready. The electrician has to go in before the walls are closed up.

Planning the programme means putting these dependencies in order: for each task, what has to be finished first.

Realistic dates

A schedule that is too optimistic becomes useless after a few days. To set dates that hold, take into account:

  • how many people each gang really has;
  • when the materials arrive;
  • other trades in the same room;
  • a margin for the unexpected.

Real progress

A task planned as finished isn't necessarily finished. If you update the programme with what is really happening on site, you see straight away which works are slipping and can warn the trades coming after in time.

If the first-floor drylining finishes three days late, the decorator needs to know today, not on the morning he arrives and finds walls still to be filled.

A client kept informed

The client wants to know when the work will be finished. If they can see the dates and the progress, they ring you far less to ask how things are going.

In short

  • ✅ For each task, what has to be finished first
  • ✅ Dates based on real gangs, materials and clashes
  • ✅ Programme updated with actual progress
  • ✅ Subcontractors and client warned in time

A good programme doesn't prevent every delay. It lets you see it coming sooner.
