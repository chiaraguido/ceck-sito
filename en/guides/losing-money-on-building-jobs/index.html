How to stop losing money on building jobs without noticing

A job can finish on time, be handed over to the client and invoiced in full. And still have made far less than you expected. Most firms losing money on building jobs never see one big mistake.

The margin rarely goes on one big error. It goes in small pieces: two operatives standing idle for half an hour, a task to redo, an extra trip to the merchant, a forgotten material. One by one they look like nothing. Added up, they change the result of the job. Here is where the hidden costs of a building job sit, and how to see them while the job is still open.

1. Extra hours

Labour is almost always the biggest cost. It doesn't take much to overrun:

What does a lost hour cost? Gang of 5 operatives, labour cost of €30 an hour.
Each loses half an hour a day → 2.5 hours a day
Over 20 working days → 50 hours × €30 = €1,500 lost, without anyone noticing.

Checking only works if hours are tied to the room and the task, and if you compare them with hours allowed during the job. We explain it in the guide to construction timesheets.

2. The gang standing idle

Paid operatives who are waiting are a full cost with no work in return. Typical causes:

  • materials not delivered or ordered late;
  • room not ready because another trade hasn't finished;
  • missing drawings or decisions not taken;
  • trips to the merchant for forgotten parts.

Ordering materials in time and knowing every day which rooms are free costs far less than a gang standing idle for even half a day.

3. Rework

Every job redone is paid for twice: hours and materials. And it often comes from wrong information: the defect badly described, the location unclear, the wrong person called to fix it.

A photo, the exact spot on the floor plan and a named person responsible avoid most of this back and forth. See how to run a snagging list.

4. Unpaid variations

"While you're at it, move this socket." The client asks verbally, the gang does it, nobody writes it down. At the end of the job that work is impossible to prove and therefore to get paid for.

  • write down every change straight away, even small ones, with date and photo;
  • log the hours used for that change separately;
  • get the client to confirm it before or straight after.

The same goes for delays caused by others: if you write them in the daily site report on the same day, you have the evidence to claim what you are owed.

5. Wasted materials

Double orders, too much offcut waste, materials ruined because they were left in the rain, leftovers nobody takes back to the yard. These are costs that don't show on any invoice, but they are there.

Keep a single list of orders per job and compare the quantities ordered with those in the quote.

6. A quote that was wrong from the start

Sometimes the job doesn't lose money: it was already losing money on the day it was signed. That happens when the hours allowed were guessed. Actual hours from finished jobs are the best basis for the next quotes: if you have logged them properly, you know what a square metre of each task really costs you.

What you need to know while the job is still open

To step in in time, every week you should have to hand:

  • hours allowed and hours used;
  • tasks finished;
  • hold-ups;
  • missing materials;
  • variations;
  • work to be redone.

The profit on a job is built every day, not when you send the final invoice.

In short

  • ✅ Hours by room and task, compared with hours allowed every week
  • ✅ Materials ordered in advance, so the gang never stops
  • ✅ Snags with a photo and a spot on the floor plan, so nothing is done twice
  • ✅ Every variation written down straight away, with photo and hours
  • ✅ Actual hours from finished jobs used for the next quote
