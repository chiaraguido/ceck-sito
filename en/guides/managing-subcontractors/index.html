Managing subcontractors on site: how to organise and coordinate trades

The joiner is waiting for the electrician. The electrician is waiting for the drylining fitter. The drylining fitter is waiting for materials. Meanwhile the job stands still. Managing subcontractors is about breaking that chain.

When several trades work on the same site, delays almost never come from the work itself. They come from information that doesn't arrive. Who goes in, in which room, when, and whether the work before theirs is really finished. Managing subcontractors on site is first of all this: getting the right information to the right person at the right time.

1. Who does what, and where

Each subcontractor must know exactly:

  • which tasks are theirs;
  • in which rooms or areas;
  • in what order: what comes first and what can wait.

When responsibility is not clear, two things happen, both expensive: work done twice, or work nobody does because everyone thought it was someone else's. And at the end of the job come the disputes.

2. A programme everyone can see

The programme is not just for the site manager. Subcontractors need it to organise their own gangs: if the electrician knows the second-floor chases are ready on Thursday, he won't send anyone on Tuesday.

The most important rule: when a date changes, the programme is updated straight away. An out-of-date programme is worse than no programme, because people believe it.

3. Write down what is holding up the work

If a subcontractor turns up and can't work, because materials are missing or the previous task isn't finished, it must be written down straight away: who, when, why.

Why it pays: at the end of the job, when delays are argued over, whoever logged the hold-ups day by day has the evidence. Whoever didn't has only memories, and everyone's memories are different.

4. Snags: each trade gets their own

Each subcontractor must be able to see only the snags assigned to them, with the photo and the spot on the floor plan, and say when they have fixed them. The site manager then checks and closes them. How to set up each snag properly is explained in how to run a snagging list.

5. Show the right things, not everything

Not everyone needs to see everything. The client wants to know how far the work has got and when it will finish, but doesn't need operatives' hours or internal costs. The subcontractor wants their own list, not everyone else's.

Giving each person only what they need makes the information clearer for everyone and keeps private what should stay inside the firm.

In short

  • ✅ Tasks, rooms and order clear for every subcontractor
  • ✅ Shared programme, updated when something changes
  • ✅ Hold-ups written down the same day
  • ✅ Each subcontractor sees their own snags
  • ✅ Everyone sees only what they need
